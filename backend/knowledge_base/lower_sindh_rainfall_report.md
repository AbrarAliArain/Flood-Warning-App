1. Regional Overview

Lower Sindh (also called Southern Sindh) is the coastal and delta belt of Sindh province, distinct from the
Indus-riverine northern districts that dominated headlines in the 2022 floods. It spans two very different flood
regimes side by side:

&bCuolla;stal / delta zone -- Karachi, Thatta, Sujawal, Badin: exposed to urban flash-flooding from intense monsoon

   downpours, cyclonic storms from the Arabian Sea, and Indus delta/tidal backwater flooding.

&bAurlild; / desert zone -- Mirpur Khas, Umerkot, Tharparkar: normally very low rainfall, but highly vulnerable when

   it comes -- poor drainage infrastructure and flat topography mean even moderate rain causes prolonged
   waterlogging.

2. Rainfall Climatology

Lower Sindh's baseline climate is arid to hyper-arid: nearly all rain falls in a compressed monsoon window
(July­September), and the province-wide long-term trend recorded by PMD is a slight decrease in mean annual
rainfall over recent decades -- but with rising volatility, meaning single extreme events now account for a larger
share of annual totals than steady seasonal rain.

5. Proposed 10-Year Rainfall Risk-Rating Methodology

This is a practical scoring framework AquaShield can implement to convert raw PMD station rainfall history into a
district-level flood risk rating. It does not require anything beyond a daily/monthly rainfall time series per station and
basic arithmetic -- making it feasible to compute live inside the dashboard's risk-prediction engine.

Step 1 -- Establish the baseline

For each district station, pull the last 10 complete monsoon seasons (Jun­Sep) of daily rainfall from PMD.
Compute the mean annual monsoon total and the standard deviation across those 10 years. This baseline is
what "normal" means for that specific station -- critical in Lower Sindh, where Karachi's normal (~150 mm/yr) is
nothing like Tharparkar's normal (~240 mm/yr).

Step 2 -- Score each year against its own baseline (Standardized Rainfall Index)

This mirrors the internationally used Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) approach, simplified to a single rolling 10-year window so it
stays lightweight for a hackathon-scale engine, while remaining upgradeable to a full 30-year WMO-standard SPI later without
changing the underlying data model.

Step 3 -- Combine rainfall score with exposure factors

Rainfall alone under-predicts flood risk in flat, poorly-drained districts (Badin, Sujawal, Mirpur Khas) versus
faster-draining desert terrain (Tharparkar, Umerkot) even at the same SRI band. Weight the rainfall score against
static exposure layers already suited to your risk map:

&bEulellv;ation / slope (flat deltaic land scores higher exposure than desert dunes)
&bDuisllt;ance to drain/embankment infrastructure (LBOD tail-end effect around Badin)
&bHuisllt;orical inundation frequency (use the 2010 / 2011 / 2022 event footprints as a prior)
&bPuolpl;ulation density and informal settlement share (drives impact severity, not physical flood extent)

Suggested composite: Final Risk Score = (0.5 × normalized SRI) + (0.3 × exposure index) + (0.2 ×
historical-frequency index), rescaled 0­100 for the dashboard. Treat these weights as a starting point to tune
once real PMD/NDMA data is wired in -- they are a reasonable hackathon default, not a calibrated scientific result.
6. Recommendations for Extending AquaShield to Lower Sindh

&bAudlld; per-district baselines, not one regional number. Karachi, the desert districts, and the delta districts have

   fundamentally different rainfall baselines -- a single Sindh-wide threshold will over-alert the desert and
   under-alert the delta.

&bPurlilo;ritize Badin, Sujawal and Thatta for the delta/coastal risk model -- embankment-dependent, LBOD

   tail-end drainage, and the only district in this set with a verified 2022 inundation percentage (Badin, 23.4%).

&bMuolld; el Karachi separately as an urban-drainage problem, not a riverine one -- short, extremely intense

   rainfall bursts overwhelming storm drains is a different failure mode from delta inundation, and likely needs a
   rainfall-intensity (mm/hour) trigger rather than a seasonal-total trigger.

&bTurlel;at Tharparkar/Umerkot as low-frequency, high-surprise risk -- their low baseline means the SRI method

   will correctly flag rare events as extreme even though absolute rainfall is modest compared to the delta districts.

&bWuilrl;e in live PMD station data before the demo if possible -- the figures in this report are the best available

   public compilation, but PMD's own station feeds (and NDMA/PDMA bulletins during an active event) will be more
   defensible in front of judges than static desk-research numbers.
